## Homework #1 — Managing an International Team (*A World of Difference*)

**What could or should Gavin Hales have done differently from the first meeting?**

The core problem in *A World of Difference* is that Gavin treats the international team as a functional unit from day one — he opens with the agenda, assigns tasks, and expects alignment on the spot. What he misses is that for several of his team members, a working relationship cannot be switched on like that. Trust and credibility are prerequisites for collaboration, not byproducts of it.

Here is what a culturally aware manager should have done, starting from the first meeting:

**1. Invest time in introductions — genuinely.** Not a perfunctory "let's go around the table," but a real exchange: where is each person from, what is their background, what are they proud of professionally? In many cultures (Southern European, Asian, Latin American), people need to know *who you are as a person* before they are willing to follow you. Gavin's authority as a London-based manager means very little to someone in Rome or Tokyo until it is earned relationally.

**2. Acknowledge cultural heterogeneity explicitly.** A team of people from different countries does not automatically share assumptions about how meetings work, how disagreement is expressed, or what "deadline" means. Gavin should have named this early: *"We come from different work cultures. Let's talk about how we want to run this team."* That meta-conversation, even 15 minutes long, prevents months of silent friction.

**3. Adapt his communication style.** As a British manager, Gavin likely communicates in understated, indirect ways that his team may misread as lack of urgency or authority. He should have been more deliberate — spelling out expectations, timelines, and his own management style explicitly, rather than assuming shared understanding.

**4. Create informal bonding space.** A dinner, a coffee break with no agenda, even a shared lunch — these are not soft extras. They are the environment where high-context team members build the baseline trust they need to engage fully in work. Gavin scheduling only structured meetings sends a signal: "I value your output, not you." That will silently disengage people.

**5. Check in individually, not just in the group.** Some team members will never raise concerns in a group setting. A brief one-on-one check-in after the first meeting would have surfaced tensions and misalignments before they became structural problems.

In short: Gavin should have led with relationship, then task — not the other way around.

## Homework #2 — France's GlobeSmart Profile: Five Personal Examples

France's GlobeSmart profile positions it as: moderately interdependent, leaning toward status, moderate risk tolerance, strongly direct (for criticism), and moderately task-oriented. Here are five examples from EPITA and Paris life that illustrate each dimension.

**1. Independent ↔ Interdependent (France: moderate, leaning interdependent)**

In a group project at EPITA, our professor consistently assessed us as a team first, asking "what did the group produce?" before asking about individual contributions. When I tried to explain my specific part separately, he redirected: "Yes, but what did the group decide together?" At Thriwe in India, individual deliverables were tracked meticulously — credit and blame were always personal. At EPITA, the group's coherence seemed to matter more than who did what. That collective framing is a quiet form of interdependence.

**2. Egalitarian ↔ Status (France: leaning toward status)**

French professors at EPITA use *vous* with students by default and expect students to use *vous* back — even informally. During one class, a student addressed a visiting professional by his first name without being invited to, and the room went noticeably cold. In India, academic hierarchy is also strong, but it is expressed through deference and silence. In France it is expressed through *register and form of address* — the language itself carries the status signal. At Prelude (my upcoming internship), I have already noticed that emails to Christos (VP Engineering) are signed off more formally by French staff than the casual Slack messages I might expect in a similarly-sized Indian startup.

**3. Risk ↔ Certainty (France: moderate, slight lean toward certainty)**

When I proposed a refactoring approach in a coding lab that changed the underlying architecture, my French lab partner's first instinct was to ask: "But is this in the course guidelines? Has the professor approved this structure?" In India, I would typically just build it and see. The French tendency is to want theoretical validation before experimentation — a Cartesian instinct to have the reasoning right before the action. This aligns with the course material's point about French deductive thinking: concept first, then implementation.

**4. Direct ↔ Indirect (France: direct for criticism, indirect otherwise)**

This is the dimension I find most interesting about France because of its internal contradiction. In a presentation review at EPITA, a professor interrupted mid-slide and said bluntly in front of the group: "This diagram is wrong. The architecture does not work this way." No softening, no "good effort though." But the same professor, when asked directly if he would recommend a student for a position, gave a vague, non-committal answer that took two follow-up questions to decode. France is direct when criticising ideas but indirect when navigating personal or political situations — almost the inverse of India, where criticism is softened but interpersonal intent is often stated plainly within trusted relationships.

**5. Task ↔ Relationship (France: moderately task-oriented, but lunch is sacred)**

Every French person I have met at EPITA is perfectly happy to start a class, a meeting, or a work session without social preamble. The standup starts, the lecture starts — relationship comes later. But the one ritual that is non-negotiable is *lunch*. Lunch at EPITA is not eaten at a desk; it is a 45-minute minimum, shared, unhurried. I once tried to eat a sandwich while reviewing notes before an afternoon class. Two French classmates looked at me like I had committed a minor crime. That moment crystallised something: France is task-first during working hours, but refuses to let efficiency colonise food. Lunch is its own protected category.

## Homework #3 — French Core Values (Based on the YouTube Video + Personal Experience)

The video *10 Tips for Working with French Colleagues* (Not Even French channel) reinforced several patterns I had already begun to observe at EPITA. Below are the core values that emerged, with examples.

**1. Intellectual Rigour and the Right to Question Everything (*L'esprit critique*)**

The French do not take statements at face value — they probe them. In the video, the presenter notes that French colleagues will argue with your ideas not because they dislike you, but because debate is how they show engagement. At EPITA, when I gave a technically correct answer in class, the professor's follow-up was always "But *why* is that true? What is the reasoning?" The value underneath this is a belief that ideas must be earned through logical demonstration, not just assertion. Coming from India, where deferring to the expert is common, this was initially unsettling — I kept wondering if I had done something wrong. I had not. I was just being invited to think.

**2. Pride in Language and Culture (*Fierté culturelle*)**

The video explicitly flags this: making an effort to speak French, even badly, unlocks warmth from French colleagues that English alone never will. I have experienced this directly. When I greet EPITA administrative staff with "Bonjour, est-ce que vous pouvez m'aider?" instead of switching straight to English, the interaction changes quality. There is visible appreciation — not because my French is good (it is not), but because the attempt signals respect. The underlying value is that French language and culture are not just tools; they are expressions of identity, and engaging with them, however imperfectly, is a form of respect.

**3. Work-Life Separation as a Right, Not a Perk**

The video makes a strong point: after hours, French colleagues do not expect or want work messages. This is not laziness — it is a clearly held value that personal time belongs to the person, not the employer. I noticed this at Prelude even during the internship negotiation phase: emails sent after 6pm received responses the next morning, without apology or explanation. At Thriwe, messages at 10pm were normal and expected responses within the hour. The French position is not that work is unimportant — it is that the boundary between work and personal life is a matter of dignity.

**4. Hierarchy is Real but Must be Earned, Not Just Assumed**

The video notes that the French respect hierarchy but will openly challenge a manager they do not consider intellectually credible. This is different from the Indian model, where seniority alone commands deference. In France, you must demonstrate that your reasoning is sound before your authority is accepted. At EPITA, students will politely but persistently push back on a professor whose explanation they find logically incomplete — something I rarely saw in Indian classrooms. The value underneath this is that authority is legitimate only when it is backed by competence and clear thinking.

**5. Pleasure and Quality of Life (*Joie de vivre*) as a Serious Value**

The video's final message is that the French are not difficult — they are simply unwilling to let the workplace consume everything. Good food, good conversation, and time to think are not decorative additions to life; they are its substance. At EPITA, the social dimension of the cohort — the café runs, the after-class conversations — is taken seriously by French students in a way I found surprising at first. They are not wasting time; they are investing in something they consider genuinely important. This has been one of the more personally useful things I have learned in France: that slowing down is not the opposite of being effective.
